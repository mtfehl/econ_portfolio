Binary Choice Models

Logit & Probit Models
Author

Michael Fehl

Published

January 21, 2026

Theory

Motivation: Discrete Outcomes

We now want to consider models were our outcome variable takes on discrete values. For example, whether or not I decide to purchase a good at a store is a choice of \(m=2\) mutually exclusive categories, which can be modeled using a Bernoulli RV.

There are many such cases of this phenomena; perhaps our outcome variable can take \(m=j\) categories, such as the different choice of cities to vacation in. There even exists cases such that each category has a natural hierarchy structure, such that \(m=1\) is mild and \(m=10\) is extreme, and so forth. For now, we will begin by introducing the simplest of these cases in which we have two possible categories; i.e., where \(y_i\) can only take on two possible values.

Binary Models

In the case where our outcome variable \(y_i\) can only take on two values, we say that \(y \sim Bern\), such that:

\[ y = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{w/ Probability } p \\ 0 & \text{w/ Probability } (1-p) \end{cases} \]

When we take the unconditional expectation of a discrete random variable, we use the formula:

\[ \mathbb{E}[y] = \sum_{i=1}^m y_i \cdot \mathbb{P}(y_i) \]

Since we have a binary model, we know that \(m=2\), such that:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[y] &= \sum_{i=1}^2 y_i \cdot \mathbb{P}(y_i) \\ &= y_i \mathbb{P}(y_i) + (1-y_i) (1-\mathbb{P}(y_i)) \\ &= 1 \mathbb{P}(y_i) + 0 (1-\mathbb{P}(y_i)) \\ &= \mathbb{P}(y_i) \end{aligned} \]

Similarly, for conditional expectations, we know that:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[y \mid x] &= \sum_{i=1}^2 y_i \cdot \mathbb{P}(y = i \mid x) \\ &= y_1 \mathbb{P}(y = 1 \mid x) + (1-y_1) (1-\mathbb{P}(y = 1 \mid x)) \\ &= 1 \cdot \mathbb{P}(y = 1 \mid x) + 0 (1-\mathbb{P}(y = 1 \mid x)) \\ &= \mathbb{P}(y = 1 \mid x) \end{aligned} \]

Modeling

In regression analysis, we write our model as:

\[ y_i = F(x_i,\beta) +\mu_i \]

Where \(F(\cdot)\) is some function used to model the probability that \(y_i = 1 \mid x\). Our specification of \(F(\cdot)\) controls the characteristics of our binary choice models.

If we assumed that \(\mathbb{E}[\mu_i \mid x] = 0\) take conditional expectations of our model, we find the following relationship:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}[y_i \mid x] = F(x_i,\beta) + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\mu_i \mid x]}_{=0} \\ &\mathbb{E}[y_i \mid x] = \mathbb{P}[y_i = 1 \mid x] = F(x_i,\beta) \end{aligned} \]

Linear Probability Model

Suppose that we decide to give \(F(\cdot)\) a standard linear form, such that the regressors have a linear relationship with the outcome variable (think standard OLS model) After applying OLS, this is known as the Linear Probability Model.

\[ F(x_i,\beta) = x_i'\beta \quad \text{such that} \quad y_i = x_i'\beta + \mu_i \] When we apply OLS, we get that:

\[ \mathbb{E}[y_i \mid x] = \mathbb{P}[y_i = 1 \mid x] = x_i'\beta \]

\[ \text{MEs:} \quad \frac{\partial \mathbb{P}[y_i = 1 \mid x]}{\partial x_i} = \beta \]

Issues

Although the LPM has nice interpretability such as the marginal effect simply being the beta coefficients, this is not a good choice to model our binary outcome variable. Specifically, there are three main reasons why linear modeling using OLS is a poor choice:

  1. Discrete Variance of Errors

We know that \(y_i\) can take on values of either \(0\) or \(1\); this implies there exists two possible values for the errors:

(i). \(y_i = 1: \mu_i = 1 - x_i'\beta\) with probability \(x_i'\beta\) (ii). \(y_i = 0: \mu_i = - x_i'\beta\) with probability \(1 - x_i'\beta\)

This implies that the error term is a discrete random variable, only able to take on two values.

  1. Unbounded Probability

We know that by definition, \(0 \leq \mathbb{P}[y_i =1 \mid x] \leq 1\); yet, we find that \(\mathbb{P}[y_i =1 \mid x] = x_i'\beta \in (-\infty, +\infty)\). Thus, we have an issue that our probabilities are bounded, yet our fitted values can lead to predictions outside of the \([0,1]\) range. This can lead to negative predicted probabilities and probabilities above 1, which are meaningless in this model.

  1. Heteroskedastic Errors

The issue with the error term from (1) is that the variance of the error terms assume a heteroskedastic structure. To see this:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{V}\text{ar}( \mu_i \mid x) &= \mathbb{E}[\mu_i^2 \mid x] - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\mu_i \mid x]^2}_\text{=0} \\ &= \mathbb{E}[(\mu_i = -x_i'\beta)^2 \mid x ] \mathbb{P}[\mu_i = -x_i'\beta \mid x] + \mathbb{E}[(\mu_i = 1-x_i'\beta)^2 \mid x ] \mathbb{P}[\mu_i = 1-x_i'\beta \mid x] \\ &= (-x_i'\beta)^2(1-p_i) + (1 - x_i'\beta)^2 (p_i) \\ &= (-x_i'\beta)^2(1-x_i'\beta) + (1-x_i'\beta)^2(x_i'\beta)\\ &= (x_i'\beta)(1-x_i'\beta) \\ &= \mathbb{P}[y_i = 1 \mid x] \mathbb{P}[y_i = 0 \mid x] \end{aligned} \]

Thus, the variance of the \(\mu_i\) depends on the \(x_i'\beta\); in other words, if \(p_i\) is large, then the variance of the errors is tiny. If \(p_i \approx 0.5\), the variance of the errors is large. This means the variance is non-constant across observations.

Logit & Probit

The goal is to constrain the outcome \(F(x_i,\beta) \in [0,1]\). To do so, we seek to construct a “single index model” such that \(F(\cdot)\) maps the single index \(x_i'\beta\) to \(y_i\). The most popular models use the form:

\[ \mathbb{P}[y_i = 1 \mid x] = F(x_i'\beta) \]

Where \(F(\cdot)\) is chosen as some cumulative distribution function; our choice of this distribution deteremines the properties of our model.

The two most popular choices are Logit and Probit.

Logit

\[ F(x_i'\beta) = \Lambda(x_i'\beta) = \frac{\exp(x_i'\beta)}{1 + \exp(x_i'\beta)} \]

Probit

\[ \begin{aligned} & F(x_i'\beta) = \Phi(x_i'\beta) = \int_{-\infty}^{x_i'\beta} \phi(t)dt \\ & \text{where } \phi(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{(\frac{-t^2}{2})} \end{aligned} \]

Both models are very similar in shape, however logit has slightly fatter tails. Both are monotone and increasing functions of \(x_i'\beta\); thus, whenever \(\uparrow \beta, \uparrow F(x_i'\beta)\).

However, since both models are no longer linear in \(\beta\), we cannot use OLS. Instead, we must use maximum likelihood as our estimators.
