Randomized Control Trials

Author

Michael Fehl

Published

February 19, 2026

Theory

Introduction

The use of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), also known as randomized controlled experiments, gives researchers “the ability to let questions determine the data to be obtained, instead of the data determining the questions that can be asked.” RCTs have become particularly common in labor, public, and development economics.

Suppose we want to estimate the causal effect of \(X_i\) on \(Y_i\). The answer is that there are good reasons to assign \(X_i\) to individuals at random, i.e. to give treatment to all sample members with equal probability. Random assignment circumvents the problems of identifying causal effects: by design, \(D_i\) will be independent of any other pre-treatment influences on \(Y_i\).

To see this formally, suppose \(X_i\) is a binary treatment indicator \(D_i\) and \(W_i\) is any discrete random variable. Under random assignment, \(P(D_i = d \mid W_i = w) = P(D_i = d)\) for all \(w\), so:

\[ \mathbb{E}[W_i \mid D_i = d] = \sum_w P(W_i = w \mid D_i = d) \cdot w = \sum_w P(W_i = w) \cdot w = \mathbb{E}[W_i] \]

The key step is that \(P(D_i = d \mid W_i = w) = P(D_i = d)\): every individual has the same probability of being treated regardless of individual characteristics. This is not an assumption but follows directly from knowledge about the assignment process. Hence, the expected values of \(W_i\) (and \(U_i\)) are the same in treatment and control groups, and the only systematic reason for the outcome variable to differ is because of the treatment.

Estimation

Under random assignment \((Y_{1i}, Y_{0i}) \perp D_i\), the selection bias in the naive difference:

\[ \beta \equiv \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid D_i = 0] = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 1]}_{\alpha_{ATT}} + \underbrace{(\mathbb{E}[Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 0])}_{\text{selection bias} = 0} \]

vanishes since \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 1] = \mathbb{E}[Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 0] = \mathbb{E}[Y_{0i}]\). It also implies \(\alpha_{ATE} = \alpha_{ATT} = \beta\). The average treatment effect can thus be estimated by a simple linear regression of \(Y_i\) on \(D_i\):

\[ Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_D D_i + U_i \]

The OLS estimator is:

\[ \hat{\beta}_D^S = \frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i (D_i - \bar{D})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i (D_i - \bar{D})^2} \qquad \text{plim}\; \hat{\beta}_D^S = \frac{\text{Cov}(D_i, Y_i)}{\text{Var}(D_i)} \]

Working through the covariance numerator using the law of iterated expectations and the fact that \(D_i^2 = D_i\):

\[ \text{plim}\; \hat{\beta}_D^S = \frac{\text{Cov}(D_i, Y_i)}{\text{Var}(D_i)} = \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid D_i = 0] = \beta_D \]

so the OLS estimator is consistent. In the case of heterogeneous effects \(\beta_i \neq \beta_D\), one can show that under random assignment plim \(\hat{\beta}_D = \mathbb{E}(\beta_i) = ATE\).

Inference

Robust Standard Errors

Default OLS standard errors assume \(\text{Var}(Y_i \mid D_i = 1) = \text{Var}(Y_i \mid D_i = 0) = \text{Var}(U_i)\). Since the treatment may affect the variance of \(Y_i\) even if not its mean, this assumption is typically unjustified. The general formula for robust standard errors is:

\[ \widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\beta}) = \frac{1}{N}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_i x_i x_i'\right]^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_i x_i x_i' \hat{u}_i^2\right]\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_i x_i x_i'\right]^{-1} \]

An alternative is to compute the variance directly from the difference in means:

\[ \text{Var}(\bar{Y}_T - \bar{Y}_C) = \text{Var}(\bar{Y}_T) + \text{Var}(\bar{Y}_C) = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_T^2}{N_1} + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_C^2}{N_0} \]

where \(\hat{\sigma}_T^2\) and \(\hat{\sigma}_C^2\) are the sample variances within the treatment and control subsamples.

Clustered Standard Errors

When observations are grouped into clusters (e.g. students within schools), within-cluster outcomes are likely correlated due to common factors. Robust standard errors are biased in this case. The clustered variance estimator is:

\[ \widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\beta}) = \frac{1}{G}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_g X_g'X_g\right]^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_g X_g'\hat{u}_g\hat{u}_g'X_g\right]\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_g X_g'X_g\right]^{-1} \]

where \(X_g\) is the \(N_g \times K\) matrix of stacked regressor vectors within cluster \(g\), and \(\hat{u}_g\) is the vector of stacked OLS residuals within cluster \(g\). This estimator is valid as long as the number of clusters \(G\) is large enough (typically above 30).

Randomization Inference

Conventional inference is based on large-sample approximations to the true asymptotic distribution of the estimator. Randomization inference produces an exact distribution irrespective of sample size. The idea is to repeatedly assign treatment at random and each time compute a corresponding estimate. The resulting distribution captures what estimates one could possibly get under the null hypothesis that the effect is zero for all units. If the original estimate falls sufficiently into the tails of this distribution, this is evidence against the null.

Additional Regressors

Consistency

In an RCT, there is no assumption that omitted variables do not matter for the determination of \(Y_i\); the omission of variables that might influence \(Y_i\) does not prevent us from obtaining consistent estimates of the causal effect of \(D_i\) on \(Y_i\). Randomization ensures that \(D_i\) is independent of all other variables, so omitted variable bias does not arise.

Denote by \(\hat{\beta}_D^L\) the OLS estimator of \(\beta_D\) in the long regression \(Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_D D_i + \beta_W W_i + U_i\). If \(D_i\) is randomized:

\[ \text{plim}\; \hat{\beta}_D^L = \beta_D \qquad \text{plim}\; \hat{\beta}_W = \beta_W + \frac{\text{Cov}(W_i, U_i)}{\text{Var}(W_i)} \]

Including additional regressors does not affect the consistency of the treatment effect estimate. However, \(\hat{\beta}_W\) will not provide a consistent estimate of \(\beta_W\) unless \(\text{Cov}(W_i, U_i) = 0\).

Improved Precision

An important advantage of including additional regressors is that it typically reduces the standard error of the estimated treatment effect. If \(D_i\) is randomized, the asymptotic variance of \(\hat{\beta}_D^L\) (with \(W_i\) included) is weakly lower than that of \(\hat{\beta}_D^S\) (without \(W_i\)). The variance will be strictly lower if \(W_i\) is a relevant predictor of \(Y_i\):

\(\beta_W = 0\) \(\beta_W \neq 0\)
\(\sigma_{DW} = 0\) \(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_D^S) = \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_D^L)\) \(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_D^S) > \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_D^L)\)
\(\sigma_{DW} \neq 0\) \(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_D^S) < \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_D^L)\) \(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_D^S) \lessgtr \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_D^L)\)

If \(D_i\) is not randomized, then the net effect on precision is ambiguous.

Balance Checks

In many real-world RCTs there are serious questions about how well the randomization was implemented in practice. One way to assess this is to check that the treatment variable is uncorrelated with the \(W_i\) variables. There are several ways to do this:

  • Compare the means of \(W_i\) in treatment and control groups.
  • Run a regression of \(D_i\) on the \(W_i\) variables and test whether the coefficients are jointly zero.
  • Compare the estimates of the treatment effect when including and excluding \(W_i\). By the OVB formula, plim \((\hat{\beta}_D^S - \hat{\beta}_D^L) = \beta_W \frac{\text{Cov}(D_i, W_i)}{\text{Var}(D_i)}\); randomization implies this should be zero.

Stratified Randomization

Randomization ensures that \(D_i\) is independent of \(W_i\) in large samples, but in a small sample the correlation between \(D_i\) and \(W_i\) may not be exactly zero. We can guarantee balance by randomizing conditional on \(W_i\), i.e. \(P(D_i = 1 \mid W_i = w) = P(D_i = 1)\) for all \(w\). By equalizing treatment probabilities within each stratum defined by \(W_i\), we set the sample correlation between \(D_i\) and \(W_i\) to zero for any sample size.

When treatment probabilities are necessarily different for different subgroups (conditional randomization), \(D_i\) is no longer independent of \(X_i\) and the simple difference estimator may suffer from omitted variable bias. However, if we control for \(X_i\) in the regression, then \(D_i\) will be independent of all other omitted variables and we get a consistent estimate of the causal effect. One can show that conditional randomization implies \(\mathbb{E}(U_i \mid D_i, X_i) = \mathbb{E}(U_i \mid X_i)\), the conditional mean independence assumption.

Heterogeneity in Treatment Effects

So far we have assumed that the treatment effect is the same for everyone. Let \(\beta_i\) denote the individual-specific treatment effect, and decompose it as:

\[ \beta_i = \mathbb{E}(\beta_i \mid W_i) + r_i = \beta_1 + \beta_2 W_i + r_i \]

where \(\mathbb{E}(r_i \mid W_i) = 0\). The ATE for subgroup \(W_i = w\) is then \(\mathbb{E}(\beta_i \mid W_i = w) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 w\), and the unconditional ATE is \(\mathbb{E}(\beta_i) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \mathbb{E}(W_i)\).

To estimate treatment effects for different levels of \(W_i\), include \(W_i\) and its interaction with \(D_i\) in the regression:

\[ Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 D_i + \beta_2 D_i W_i + \beta_3 W_i + U_i \]

As long as \(D_i\) is randomized, the OLS estimators of \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) will be unbiased and consistent. Including \(W_i\) as a separate regressor is crucial to obtain consistent estimates of \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\).

Partial or Imperfect Compliance

Setup

In practice, individuals in the treatment group often cannot be forced to actually take the treatment they have been assigned. Similarly, individuals in the control group may manage to get the treatment. Let \(D_i\) be an indicator for actual receipt of the treatment and \(Z_i\) an indicator for being assigned to the treatment group.

  • No-shows: \(Z_i = 1\) but \(D_i = 0\) (assigned to treatment, did not receive it)
  • Cross-overs: \(Z_i = 0\) but \(D_i = 1\) (not assigned to treatment, received it anyway)

With imperfect compliance, \(D_i\) is likely correlated with \(U_i\), so OLS on the outcome equation is biased. Writing the first stage as \(D_i = \pi_0 + \pi_Z Z_i + V_i\), we have:

\[ \text{plim}\; \hat{\beta}_D^{OLS} = \beta_D + \frac{\text{Cov}(V_i, U_i)}{\text{Var}(D_i)} \neq \beta_D \]

Intention-to-Treat Effect

Since \(Z_i\) is randomized, we can get an unbiased estimate of the intention-to-treat effect (ITT) as the difference in average outcomes between treatment and control groups:

\[ ITT = \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0] = \beta_D \pi_Z + \beta_Z \]

where \(\beta_Z\) is the effect of being assigned to the treatment group on outcome \(Y_i\) that is unrelated to \(Z_i\)’s impact on the probability of getting the treatment. The ITT is not the causal effect \(\beta_D\); it is attenuated compared to \(\beta_D\) since \(\pi_Z < 1\).

However, if being assigned to either group has no effect on the outcome in itself (exclusion restriction: \(\beta_Z = 0\)), we can use \(Z_i\) as an instrument for \(D_i\).

Wald Estimand

A common solution for imperfect compliance is to use assignment \(Z_i\) as an instrument for treatment receipt \(D_i\). In the exactly identified case, the IV estimator converges to the Wald estimand:

\[ \text{plim}\; \hat{\beta}_D^{IV} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0]}{\mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]} \]

The denominator is the difference in the probability of receipt of treatment between treatment and control groups. With perfect compliance this is equal to 1 but with partial compliance it will be less than 1. Note that the Wald estimand can also be written as a ratio of the reduced form (ITT) to the first stage: \(ITT = ATT \times P(D_i = 1 \mid Z_i = 1)\).

What Does the Wald Estimand Identify?

The parameter identified by the Wald estimand depends on whether treatment effects are homogeneous and on the nature of deviations from randomization:

A1 Constant treatment effect and compliance: \(\beta_i = \beta_D\), \(\pi_i = \pi_Z\)

A2 Independence of \(Z_i\): \(Z_i \perp U_i, V_i\) (and \(Z_i \perp \beta_i, \pi_i\) if A1 fails)

A3 Exclusion restriction: \(\beta_Z = 0\)

A4 First stage: \(\mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 0] \neq 0\)

Under A1-A4 (homogeneous effects, full and two-sided non-compliance), the Wald estimand identifies \(\beta_D\).

Under A2-A4 with heterogeneous effects (dropping A1), the Wald estimand identifies a weighted average treatment effect (WATE):

\[ \frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0]}{\mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]} = \frac{\mathbb{E}(\beta_i \pi_i)}{\mathbb{E}(\pi_i)} = \mathbb{E}\left(\beta_i \frac{\pi_i}{\mathbb{E}(\pi_i)}\right) \]

The WATE equals the ATE only if \(\pi_i = \pi_Z\) for all \(i\) or \(\text{Cov}(\beta_i, \pi_i) = 0\).

Under A2-A4 with one-sided non-compliance (no cross-overs, only no-shows) and effect heterogeneity, the Wald estimand identifies the ATT:

\[ \frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0]}{\mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]} = \mathbb{E}(\beta_i \mid D_i = 1) \]

LATE and Complier Types

Under an additional monotonicity assumption (A5: \(P(\pi_i = -1) = 0\), ruling out defiers), the Wald estimand identifies the local average treatment effect (LATE) for compliers \(\mathbb{E}(\beta_i \mid \pi_i = 1)\):

There are four latent types of individuals:

\(Z_i\) \(D_i\) \(D_{1i}\) \(D_{0i}\) \(\pi_i\) Latent Type Observed Type
1 1 1 1 0 Always-taker
1 1 1 0 1 Complier
1 0 0 1 -1 Defier No-show
1 0 0 0 0 Never-taker No-show
0 1 1 1 0 Always-taker Cross-over
0 1 0 1 -1 Defier Cross-over
0 0 1 0 1 Complier
0 0 0 0 0 Never-taker

Always-takers get treatment regardless of assignment. Never-takers never get treatment regardless. Compliers get treatment if and only if assigned to the treatment group (\(\pi_i = 1\)). Defiers do the opposite of their assignment (\(\pi_i = -1\)). Monotonicity rules out defiers.

Under A2-A5:

\[ \frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0]}{\mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]} = \mathbb{E}(\beta_i \mid \pi_i = 1) = \text{LATE} \]

The proportion of compliers in the population can be quantified by the first stage: \(P(\pi_i = 1) = \mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - \mathbb{E}[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]\). The larger the group of compliers, the closer the LATE will be to the ATE.

The three treatment effect parameters are related as follows:

\[ \mathbb{E}(\beta_i) = P(D_i = 1)\mathbb{E}(\beta_i \mid D_i = 1) + P(D_i = 0)\mathbb{E}(\beta_i \mid D_i = 0) \]

The ATT can be decomposed as a probability-weighted sum of the LATE, the average effect on always-takers, and the average effect on defiers. Under monotonicity (no defiers), and if there are also no cross-overs (no always-takers among the treated), LATE and ATT coincide.

Problems with RCTs

Despite providing convincing estimates of causal treatment effects, RCTs face several practical challenges:

  • Expense: RCTs are often expensive whereas non-experimental data is often available at little or no additional cost.
  • Ethical issues: Social experiments raise ethical concerns related to some people receiving treatment and others not.
  • Experimental effects: The Hawthorne and John Henry effects – the excitement of being “watched” – may bring forth greater effort that would not normally be present.
  • Non-representative sample: Experimental data can only provide estimates of average treatment effects for those in the experiment. One cannot necessarily generalize conclusions to other populations.
  • Non-representative program: A particular program will not necessarily have the same impact in other places or at other times when the context might be very different.
  • General equilibrium effects: If some intervention is found to be successful and is scaled up, the scale may have consequences for the effects of the intervention. For example, a program that helps individuals find work faster might have no aggregate effect on employment if the number of jobs is fixed.
