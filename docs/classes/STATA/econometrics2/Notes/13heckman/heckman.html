Heckit Estimator

Author

Michael Fehl

Published

February 5, 2026

Theory

Motivation: Incompletely Observed Outcomes

Standard OLS assumes a representative sample: all observations are drawn from the same population. Two closely related situations violate this, each requiring a different remedy.

  1. The dependent variable is incompletely observed: some values of \(y\) are missing or replaced by a boundary value. This is censoring.
  2. The dependent variable is only observed for a selected sample: entire observations are dropped based on the outcome. This is truncation.

OLS applied to censored or truncated data yields inconsistent estimates because the conditional mean is no longer linear in \(\mathbf{x}\).

Censoring and Truncation

Let \(y^*\) be the latent (true) value of the dependent variable. The observed \(y\) is a transformation \(y = g(y^*)\). The key distinction is:

Censoring (from below at \(L\)): We observe \(y^*\) when \(y^* > L\) but replace it with \(L\) when \(y^* \leq L\). The regressor data is kept for all observations.

\[ y = \begin{cases} y^* & \text{if } y^* > L \\ L & \text{if } y^* \leq L \end{cases} \]

Example: earnings data where everyone below a threshold is reported as earning exactly the threshold amount (top-coding from above is the symmetric case).

Truncation (from below at \(L\)): We only observe the data when \(y^* > L\). Observations at or below the boundary are lost entirely.

\[ y = y^* \quad \text{if } y^* > L \quad \text{(only these are in the sample)} \]

Example: sampling only employed workers. Truncation entails greater information loss than censoring since the regressors \(\mathbf{x}\) are also lost for out-of-sample observations.

Censored MLE

Let \(f^*(y \mid \mathbf{x})\) and \(F^*(y \mid \mathbf{x})\) be the pdf and cdf of the latent \(y^*\). Define the censoring indicator \(d_i = \mathbf{1}[y_i > L]\). Then the conditional density of the observed \(y\) is:

\[ f(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = f^*(y \mid \mathbf{x})^d \cdot F^*(L \mid \mathbf{x})^{1-d} \]

The censored MLE maximizes:

\[ \ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \left[d_i \ln f^*(y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i, \theta) + (1-d_i) \ln F^*(L_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i, \theta)\right] \]

where \(\theta\) collects the parameters of the distribution of \(y^*\). The censored MLE is consistent and asymptotically normal provided \(f^*(y^* \mid \mathbf{x})\) is correctly specified.

Truncated MLE

For truncation from below, the observed \(y\) has conditional density:

\[ f(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{f^*(y \mid \mathbf{x})}{\Pr[y > L \mid \mathbf{x}]} = \frac{f^*(y \mid \mathbf{x})}{1 - F^*(L \mid \mathbf{x})} \]

The truncated MLE maximizes:

\[ \ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \left[\ln f^*(y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i, \theta) - \ln\!\left(1 - F^*(L_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i, \theta)\right)\right] \]

The Tobit Model

The Tobit model (Tobin, 1958) is the most common censored regression model. It assumes that the latent variable is linear in \(\mathbf{x}\) with normally distributed errors, and censoring occurs from below at zero:

\[ y_i^* = \mathbf{x}_i'\beta + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) \]

\[ y_i = \begin{cases} y_i^* & \text{if } y_i^* > 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } y_i^* \leq 0 \end{cases} \]

So \(y_i^* \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i'\beta, \sigma^2)\). Let \(w_i = \mathbf{x}_i'\beta/\sigma\) for shorthand. The Tobit MLE is inconsistent if \(\varepsilon_i\) is either heteroskedastic or non-normal.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Using the general censored MLE with \(f^* = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i'\beta, \sigma^2)\), the observed density indicator \(d_i = \mathbf{1}[y_i > 0]\), and \(F^*(0 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = 1 - \Phi(w_i)\):

\[ \ell(\beta, \sigma^2) = \sum_{i=1}^N \left\{d_i\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\ln 2\pi - \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\sigma^2 - \frac{(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i'\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right] + (1-d_i)\ln\!\left[1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}_i'\beta}{\sigma}\right)\right]\right\} \]

The Tobit MLE \(\hat\theta = (\hat\beta', \hat\sigma^2)'\) maximizes this.

First Order Conditions

Differentiating with respect to \(\beta\) and \(\sigma^2\):

\[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta} = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\left(d_i(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i'\beta) - (1-d_i)\frac{\sigma\phi_i}{1-\Phi_i}\right)\mathbf{x}_i = 0 \]

\[ \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\sigma^2} = \sum_{i=1}^N \left\{d_i\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{(y_i - \mathbf{x}_i'\beta)^2}{2\sigma^4}\right) + (1-d_i)\frac{\phi_i\,\mathbf{x}_i'\beta}{(1-\Phi_i)\,2\sigma^3}\right\} = 0 \]

where \(\phi_i = \phi(w_i)\) and \(\Phi_i = \Phi(w_i)\).

Marginal Effects

The key insight is that the marginal effect of a regressor depends on which conditional mean we care about. Let \(w = \mathbf{x}'\beta/\sigma\) and define the inverse Mills ratio \(\lambda(z) = \phi(z)/\Phi(z)\) (derived below).

Quantity Mean \(\partial/\partial\mathbf{x}\)
Latent variable \(E[y^* \mid \mathbf{x}] = \mathbf{x}'\beta\) \(\beta\)
Truncated mean \(E[y \mid y > 0, \mathbf{x}]\) \(\left\{1 - w\lambda(w) - \lambda(w)^2\right\}\beta\)
Censored mean \(E[y \mid \mathbf{x}]\) \(\Phi(w)\,\beta\)

The factor \(\Phi(w)\) in the censored marginal effect is the probability that the observation is uncensored; at zero censoring (\(w \to \infty\)), \(\Phi(w) \to 1\) and the Tobit coefficient recovers the OLS coefficient. The censored marginal effect is the one reported in standard applied work.

Sample Selection Models

The Bivariate Sample Selection Model

The simple Tobit model assumes the selection mechanism (who is observed) and the outcome equation share the same latent variable. The bivariate sample selection model (also called the Type 2 or generalized Tobit) separates them into two distinct equations:

Participation equation (determines whether \(y_2\) is observed):

\[ y_1 = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } y_1^* > 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } y_1^* \leq 0 \end{cases}, \qquad y_1^* = \mathbf{x}_1'\beta_1 + \varepsilon_1 \]

Outcome equation (observed only when \(y_1 = 1\)):

\[ y_2 = \begin{cases} y_2^* & \text{if } y_1^* > 0 \\ - & \text{if } y_1^* \leq 0 \end{cases}, \qquad y_2^* = \mathbf{x}_2'\beta_2 + \varepsilon_2 \]

Example: \(y_1^*\) determines whether a person works at all (participation); \(y_2^*\) determines how many hours they work conditional on working. Different factors may drive each decision (e.g., fixed costs of commuting affect the participation margin but not hours conditional on working).

Joint Normal Errors

Estimation requires an assumption on the joint distribution of \((\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2)\). The standard assumption is joint normality with homoskedastic errors:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon_1 \\ \varepsilon_2\end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix},\, \begin{bmatrix}1 & \sigma_{12} \\ \sigma_{12} & \sigma_2^2\end{bmatrix}\right) \]

The normalization \(\sigma_1^2 = 1\) is imposed since only the sign of \(y_1^*\) is observed; rescaling \(y_1^*\) by any \(a > 0\) does not change the observed \(y_1\), so the variance is not identified.

Conditional Means

Inverse Mills Ratio

Using the truncated normal distribution, for any \(\varepsilon_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\):

\[ \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_1 \mid \varepsilon_1 > -c] = \frac{\phi(c)}{\Phi(c)} \equiv \lambda(c) \]

The function \(\lambda(z) = \phi(z)/\Phi(z)\) is the inverse Mills ratio. It is always positive and decreasing in \(z\).

Truncated Conditional Mean

The mean of \(y_2\) for the observed (selected) subsample is:

\[ \mathbb{E}[y_2 \mid \mathbf{x}, y_1^* > 0] = \mathbf{x}_2'\beta_2 + \sigma_{12}\lambda(\mathbf{x}_1'\beta_1) \]

This follows from writing \(\varepsilon_2 = \sigma_{12}\varepsilon_1 + \xi\) where \(\xi\) is independent of \(\varepsilon_1\), so \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_2 \mid \varepsilon_1 > -\mathbf{x}_1'\beta_1] = \sigma_{12}\lambda(\mathbf{x}_1'\beta_1)\).

The key implication: OLS on only the observed \(y_2\) is inconsistent unless \(\sigma_{12} = 0\) (the errors are uncorrelated), because the inverse Mills ratio acts as an omitted variable correlated with \(\mathbf{x}_2\).

Censored Mean

If \(y_2 = 0\) when \(y_1^* \leq 0\), the unconditional mean is:

\[ \mathbb{E}[y_2 \mid \mathbf{x}] = \Phi(\mathbf{x}_1'\beta_1)\,\mathbf{x}_2'\beta_2 + \sigma_{12}\phi(\mathbf{x}_1'\beta_1) \]

The Heckman Two-Step Estimator

The full MLE for the bivariate sample selection model is efficient but computationally demanding. Heckman (1979) proposed a simpler two-step approach, known as the Heckit estimator.

Step 1: Estimate a Probit model for selection on the full sample (all \(N\) observations, including \(y_1 = 0\)). Since \(\Pr(y_1 = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_1) = \Phi(\mathbf{x}_1'\beta_1)\), this yields consistent estimates \(\hat\beta_1\). Compute the estimated inverse Mills ratio for each individual:

\[ \hat\lambda_i = \lambda(\mathbf{x}_{1i}'\hat\beta_1) = \frac{\phi(\mathbf{x}_{1i}'\hat\beta_1)}{\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{1i}'\hat\beta_1)} \]

Step 2: Using only the selected subsample (\(y_{1i} = 1\)), run OLS of \(y_{2i}\) on \(\mathbf{x}_{2i}\) and \(\hat\lambda_i\):

\[ y_{2i} = \mathbf{x}_{2i}'\beta_2 + \sigma_{12}\hat\lambda_i + v_i \]

The coefficient on \(\hat\lambda_i\) estimates \(\sigma_{12}\). A \(t\)-test of this coefficient is a test for sample selection (\(H_0: \sigma_{12} = 0\)).

Note: The standard errors from Step 2 OLS are incorrect. They must be adjusted to account for both heteroskedasticity and the fact that \(\hat\lambda_i\) was estimated in Step 1 (sampling variability of \(\hat\beta_1\) carries through). Stata’s heckman, twostep handles this automatically.

The Heckit estimator is less efficient than MLE under joint normality but is simpler to implement and more robust: it only requires \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_2 \mid \varepsilon_1] = \delta\varepsilon_1\) rather than full joint normality.

Identification

The bivariate sample selection model is in principle identified without any restrictions on the regressors, purely through the functional form of \(\lambda(\cdot)\). However, the inverse Mills ratio \(\lambda(\mathbf{x}_1'\beta_1)\) is approximately linear over a wide range of its argument. If \(\mathbf{x}_1 = \mathbf{x}_2\), this creates severe multicollinearity between \(\hat\lambda_i\) and \(\mathbf{x}_{2i}\) in Step 2.

In practice, reliable identification requires an exclusion restriction: at least one variable in \(\mathbf{x}_1\) (the participation equation) that is excluded from \(\mathbf{x}_2\) (the outcome equation). This instrument must affect selection but have no direct effect on \(y_2^*\) conditional on participation.

* --- Tobit (censored from below at 0 by default) ---

tobit depvar indepvars, ll(0) ul(100000)

* --- alternatively (more general) ---
intreg depvar_lower depvar_upper indepvars

* --- marginal effects: censored mean (default) ---
margins, dydx(*)

* --- marginal effects: truncated mean ---
margins, dydx(*) predict(e(0,.))

* --- marginal effects: latent variable ---
margins, dydx(*) predict(xb)

* --- Heckman two-step (Heckit) ---
* depvar   = outcome variable (y2)
* indepvars = regressors in outcome equation (x2)
* select() = selection equation: lhs is 0/1 indicator (y1),
*            rhs contains selection regressors (x1, must include exclusion restriction)
heckman depvar indepvars, select(y1 = x1 exclusion_var) twostep

* --- Heckman MLE (more efficient under joint normality) ---
heckman depvar indepvars, select(y1 = x1 exclusion_var)

* reported automatically in heckman output as "rho" test
