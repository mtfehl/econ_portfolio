First Differences

Author

Michael Fehl

Published

January 12, 2026

Theory

Motivation: Alternative Error Covariance Structure

The FE estimator eliminates \(f_i\) by subtracting individual-specific time means. First differencing is an alternative: subtract consecutive observations rather than group means. Both transformations remove \(f_i\) and yield consistent estimators under the same exogeneity assumption, but they impose different homoskedasticity conditions and differ in efficiency depending on the structure of \(\mu_{it}\).

Under the FE homoskedasticity assumption (\(\mu_{it}\) i.i.d. over \(t\)), first differencing introduces MA(1) serial correlation into \(\Delta\mu_{it}\) and FE is more efficient. Under the FD homoskedasticity assumption (\(\Delta\mu_{it}\) i.i.d., i.e., \(\mu_{it}\) follows a random walk), FD is the more efficient estimator. When \(T = 2\), the two estimators are numerically identical.

Assumptions

1. Linearity

Same as before:

\[ y_{it} = \mathbf{x}_{it}'\beta + \mathbf{z}_{i}'\gamma + f_i + \mu_{it} \]

2. Fixed Individual Effects

Same as FE:

\[ E(f_i \mid X_i) \neq 0 \]

\(f_i\) can be correlated with the regressors and is eliminated by first differencing.

3. Strictly Exogenous Regressors

Same as before:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\mu_{it} \mid \mathbf{x}_{i1}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_{iT}, f_i] \equiv \mathbb{E}[\mu_{it} \mid X_i, f_i] = 0 \]

4. Homoskedasticity (After First Differencing)

NEW. The homoskedasticity condition is imposed on the first-differenced error \(\Delta\mu_{it} = \mu_{it} - \mu_{i,t-1}\):

\[ \begin{cases} 1. \ \mathbb{V}\text{ar}(\Delta\mu_{it} \mid X_i, f_i) = \sigma_{e}^2 & \text{Constant Variance After Differencing} \\ 2. \ \mathbb{C}\text{ov}(\Delta\mu_{it}, \Delta\mu_{is} \mid X_i, f_i) = 0 \quad \forall_{t \neq s} & \text{No Serial Correlation After Differencing} \end{cases} \]

\(\star\) This implies \(\mu_{it} = \mu_{i,t-1} + e_{it}\) where \(e_{it}\) is i.i.d. with variance \(\sigma_e^2\); in other words, \(\mu_{it}\) follows a random walk. This is qualitatively different from the FE homoskedasticity assumption and is not nested in it (for \(T > 2\)).

5. Linearly Independent Regressors (After Differencing)

NEW. The rank condition is imposed on the first-differenced regressors \(\Delta X_i\):

\[ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i \xrightarrow{p} \underbrace{\mathbb{E}(\Delta X_i'\Delta X_i)}_{M_{\Delta X \Delta X}: \text{ (K x K)}} \text{ has rank } K^* \]

Where \(K^*\) represents the number of time-varying regressors. Time-constant regressors satisfy \(\Delta z_i = 0\) and cannot enter the first-differenced model; like FE, FD cannot identify the coefficients on time-invariant covariates.

Implications: The First-Difference Operator

Taking first differences of the model for \(t = 2, \ldots, T\):

\[ \underbrace{y_{it} - y_{i,t-1}}_{\Delta y_{it}} = \underbrace{(\mathbf{x}_{it} - \mathbf{x}_{i,t-1})'}_{\Delta\mathbf{x}_{it}'}\beta + \underbrace{(\mathbf{z}_{i} - \mathbf{z}_{i})}_{=0}'\gamma + \underbrace{(f_i - f_i)}_{=0} + \underbrace{(\mu_{it} - \mu_{i,t-1})}_{\Delta\mu_{it}} \]

since \(\Delta f_i = f_i - f_i = 0\) and \(\Delta \mathbf{z}_{i} = \mathbf{z}_{i} - \mathbf{z}_{i} =0\). In matrix notation, the \((T-1) \times T\) first-difference matrix is:

\[ D_T = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

so that \(\Delta\mathbf{y}_i = D_T\mathbf{y}_i\), \(\Delta X_i = D_T X_i\), and \(\Delta\mu_i = D_T\mu_i\). We lose one time period per individual, giving \(N(T-1)\) total observations.

The FD estimator is OLS applied to the first-differenced model:

\[ \hat{\beta}_{FD} = \left(\sum_{i=1}^N \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^N \Delta X_i'\Delta\mathbf{y}_i\right) \]

Properties of the FD Estimator

Unbiasedness

The sampling error is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\beta}_{FD} - \beta &= \left(\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i\right)^{-1}\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta\mathbf{y}_i - \beta \\ &= \left(\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i\right)^{-1}\sum_i \Delta X_i'(\Delta X_i\beta + \Delta\mu_i) - \beta \\ &= \left(\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i\right)^{-1}\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta\mu_i \end{aligned} \]

Taking conditional expectations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{FD} - \beta \mid X, f] &= \left(\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i\right)^{-1}\sum_i \Delta X_i' \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mu_i \mid X_i, f_i]}_{\text{=0 via A3}} = 0 \\ \Rightarrow \mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{FD}] &= \beta \qquad \blacksquare \end{aligned} \]

Consistency

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\beta}_{FD} - \beta &= \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i\right)^{-1}}_{(1)} \underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta\mu_i}_{(2)} \\ (1) &\xrightarrow{p} M_{\Delta X \Delta X}^{-1} \text{ via LLN and CMT} \\ (2) &\xrightarrow{p} \mathbb{E}[\Delta X_i'\Delta\mu_i] = 0 \text{ via LLN and strict exogeneity (A3)} \\ \hat{\beta}_{FD} &\xrightarrow{p} \beta \qquad \blacksquare \end{aligned} \]

Asymptotic Normality

\[ \begin{aligned} \sqrt{N}(\hat{\beta}_{FD} - \beta) &= \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i\right)^{-1}}_{(1)} \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta\mu_i}_{(2)} \\ (1) &\xrightarrow{p} M_{\Delta X \Delta X}^{-1} \text{ via LLN and CMT} \\ (2) &\xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(0, \mathbb{V}\text{ar}(\Delta X_i'\Delta\mu_i)\right) \text{ via CLT} \end{aligned} \]

Under assumption 4, \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mu_i\Delta\mu_i' \mid X_i, f_i] = \sigma_e^2 I_{T-1}\), so \(\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(\Delta X_i'\Delta\mu_i) = \sigma_e^2 M_{\Delta X \Delta X}\). Thus:

\[ \sqrt{N}(\hat{\beta}_{FD} - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(0, \sigma_e^2 M_{\Delta X \Delta X}^{-1}\right) \qquad \blacksquare \]

Issue: Estimating A\(\mathbb{V}\)AR

The A\(\mathbb{V}\)AR under assumption 4 is \(\sigma_e^2 M_{\Delta X \Delta X}^{-1}\). To estimate \(\sigma_e^2\), compute FD residuals \(\hat{e}_{it} = \Delta y_{it} - \Delta\mathbf{x}_{it}'\hat{\beta}_{FD}\) and use:

\[ \hat{\sigma}_e^2 = \frac{1}{N(T-1) - K}\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{t=2}^T \hat{e}_{it}^2 \]

Using the analogy principle:

\[ \hat{\sigma}_e^2\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i\right)^{-1} \xrightarrow{p} \sigma_e^2\,\mathbb{E}[\Delta X_i'\Delta X_i]^{-1} \]

If assumption 4 fails, we instead use the cluster-robust ‘sandwich’ estimator:

\[ \widehat{\text{A}\mathbb{V}\text{ar}}(\hat{\beta}_{FD}) = \left(\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_i \Delta X_i'\hat{e}_i\hat{e}_i'\Delta X_i\right)\left(\sum_i \Delta X_i'\Delta X_i\right)^{-1} \]

\(\star\) In practice, standard errors should always be clustered at the individual level in a first-difference model, since the true structure of \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mu_i\Delta\mu_i']\) is generally unknown.

FD vs. FE

  • When \(T = 2\): FE and FD produce numerically identical estimates and inference. (to see this, check out Q3 of PS1)
  • When \(T > 2\) and \(\mu_{it}\) is i.i.d. over \(t\): FE is more efficient. Differencing introduces MA(1) serial correlation into \(\Delta\mu_{it}\), which loses information relative to the within transformation.
  • When \(T > 2\) and \(\mu_{it}\) follows a random walk: FD is more efficient, since \(\Delta\mu_{it} = e_{it}\) is then i.i.d. and assumption 4 holds exactly.
  • If FE and FD estimates differ in ways that cannot be attributed to sampling error, this signals a violation of strict exogeneity: if \(\mu_{it}\) is correlated with \(\mathbf{x}_{is}\) for any \(t\) and \(s\), both estimators are inconsistent but will generally have different probability limits.

To see the difference in FE and FD being blue under different assumptions of serial correlation of the error terms, suppose that \(\mu_{it}\)is serially uncorrelated such that \(\text{cov}(\mu_{it}, \mu_{is}) = 0, \quad \forall_{t \neq s}\).

In this case, estimating the variance-covariance matrix on the first-differenced idiosyncratic errors takes the following form:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\Delta \mu_i ' \Delta \mu_i \mid X_i] = \sigma_{\mu}^2 \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 & \ddots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

To derive this matrix, we simply calculate the variance-covariance between first-differenced errors at different \(t\):

\[ \begin{align} \text{1. Diagonal } (t=2): & \quad \mathbb{E}[\Delta \mu_{i2} \Delta \mu_{i2} \mid X_i] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[(\mu_{i2} - \mu_{i1}) (\mu_{i2} - \mu_{i1}) \mid X_i] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i2}\mu_{i2} - 2\mu_{i2}\mu_{i1} + \mu_{i1}\mu_{i1} \mid X_i] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i2}^2 \mid X_i] - 2\mathbb{E}[\mu_{i2}\mu_{i1} \mid X_i] + \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i1}^2 \mid X_i] \\ &= \sigma_{\mu}^2 - 0 + \sigma_{\mu}^2 = 2\sigma_{\mu}^2 \\ \\ \text{2. Off-Diagonal }(t=3): & \quad \mathbb{E}[\Delta \mu_{i3} \Delta \mu_{i2} \mid X_i] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[(\mu_{i3} - \mu_{i2}) (\mu_{i2} - \mu_{i1}) \mid X_i] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i3}\mu_{i2} - \mu_{i3}\mu_{i1} - \mu_{i2}\mu_{i2} + \mu_{i2}\mu_{i1} \mid X_i] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i3}\mu_{i2} \mid X_i] - \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i3}\mu_{i1} \mid X_i] - \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i2}^2 \mid X_i] + \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i2}\mu_{i1} \mid X_i] \\ &= 0 - 0 - \sigma_{\mu}^2 + 0 = -\sigma_{\mu}^2 \\ \\ \text{3. Off-Diagonal }(t \geq 4): & \quad \mathbb{E}[\Delta \mu_{i4} \Delta \mu_{i2} \mid X_i] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[(\mu_{i4} - \mu_{i3}) (\mu_{i2} - \mu_{i1}) \mid X_i] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i4}\mu_{i2} - \mu_{i4}\mu_{i1} - \mu_{i3}\mu_{i2} + \mu_{i3}\mu_{i1} \mid X_i] \\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i4}\mu_{i2} \mid X_i] - \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i4}\mu_{i1} \mid X_i] - \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i3}\mu_{i2} \mid X_i] + \mathbb{E}[\mu_{i3}\mu_{i1} \mid X_i] \\ &= 0 - 0 - 0 + 0 = 0 \end{align} \]

Thus, if \(\mu_{it}\) are i.i.d. (no serial correlation) rather than a random walk, applying FD now introduces serial correlation! Hence, FE would be BLUE.

Additional: \(\mu_{it} = \rho \mu_{it-1} + e_{it}\)

There exists a third case wherein the idiosyncratic errors are serially correlated, but do NOT follow a random walk.

Warning

In reality, it is very hard for the random walk assumption to hold in this setting; it would imply that a shock from 10-20 years ago is still perfectly persistent today, with no decay whatsoever.

More formally, \(\mu_{it} = \rho \mu_{it-1} + e_{it}\) where \(0 < \rho < 1\); this means our errors follow an AR(1) error structure (see ARMA notes for an extensive overview of AR(p) and MA(q) models). \(\rho\) is a measure of “persistence,” telling us how much a shock from last period (\(t-1\)) is carried over to today, \(t\). When \(\rho\) is between zero and one, the shock decays over time, eventually converging to zero, determined by how close the value is to zero.

In such a case, neither FD nor FE are BLUE! As a result, if we run either estimator without corrections, our standard errors will be incorrectly estimated (typically underestimated). This leads to inflated t-statistics, artificially tight confidence intervals, and a high risk of Type I errors (finding statistical significance where there is none).

While neither estimator is BLUE, their relative efficiency depends entirely on the value of \(\rho\):

  • As \(\rho \rightarrow 0\), FE becomes closer to being efficient.

  • As \(\rho \rightarrow 1\), FD becomes closer to being efficient.

  • If \(\rho \approx 0.5\), both estimators suffer from significant inefficiency.

There are two different approaches to take when dealing with such a situation:

Cluster (Robust)

In modern empirical practice, researchers generally care more about consistency than absolute efficiency, provided they can get inference right.

The idea here is to stick with the Fixed Effects (FE) estimator (or FD, if \(\rho\) is empirically very high), but now estimate the model using clustered (robust) standard errors.

Clustering here allows the variance-covariance matrix to be completely agnostic about the exact nature of the serial correlation within an pre-defined cluster. It corrects the standard errors for any pattern of autocorrelation (and heteroskedasticity) over time.

FGLS (BLUE)

To strictly achieve BLUE (or asymptotically efficient estimates), you must explicitly model the AR(1) process using Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS). The process is multi-step:

  1. Estimate the model using FE to get consistent residuals.
  2. Use those residuals to get \(\hat{\rho}\).
  3. Apply a quasi-differencing transforatmion to the panel data. Instead of subtracting 100% of the previous period (like FD) or the total average (like FE), you subtract \(\hat{\rho}\) of the previous period:

\[ y_{it}− \hat{\rho} y_{it-1} = (x_{it}−\hat{\rho}x_{it−1})'\beta + f_i(1−\hat{\rho})+e_{it} \]

Note: FGLS requires strict assumptions about the exact structure of the error variance. If your estimate of \(\rho\) is wrong, or if the true process is actually AR(2) or MA(1), FGLS can perform worse than FE.

Applied

#| eval: false

* --- declare panel structure ---
xtset id year

* --- first difference estimator ---
* Stata's d. prefix applies the first-difference operator
* noconstant is required: the within-individual mean of the differenced model is zero
reg d.y d.x1 d.x2 d.x3, noconstant

* --- FD with cluster-robust standard errors ---
* the true structure of E[Delta_u_i Delta_u_i'] is generally unknown,
* so clustering at the individual level is standard practice
reg d.y d.x1 d.x2 d.x3, noconstant vce(cluster id)

* --- FD with year dummies (time fixed effects) ---
* first-differencing year dummies removes the constant, leaving T-2 year indicators
reg d.y d.x1 d.x2 d.x3 d.i.year, noconstant vce(cluster id)

* --- compare FE and FD point estimates ---
* if T=2 these should be identical; for T>2 differences signal serial correlation
xtreg y x1 x2 x3, fe vce(cluster id)
estimates store fe_est

reg d.y d.x1 d.x2 d.x3, noconstant vce(cluster id)
estimates store fd_est

esttab fe_est fd_est, mtitles("Fixed Effects" "First Difference")
