Identifying, Validating, and Selecting ARIMA models.
Author

Michael Fehl, Francesco Zucca, Giuseppe Lista

Published

February 16, 2026

1 Introduction

In this practice, we will work on identifying \(ARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q)_s\) models for non-stationary time series that have a seasonal component. We will also focus on validating models fitted to time series data and selecting the best model among different alternatives. The chosen model will then be used to generate forecasts. As done in previous practices, each student group must follow the practice script below, per- forming the necessary calculations and graphs in the R environment. The obtained results and answers to the questions should be compiled into a document containing your solution, created, for example, in R Markdown or Microsoft Word. Please make sure to include your full names and group number at the beginning of the document. Submit the questionnaire solution in PDF format by uploading it to the platform under the corresponding task section before the deadline.

2.2 Exploratory Analysis

plot(unempl,main="Unemployment Rate in Spain, since 2000",
     ylab="UR (%)")
abline(v=1996:2026,col=4,lty=3)

From first glances at our data after plotting, we observe some initial characteristics of the ts.

First, that the trend seems to be non-constant; it remains constant from 2000-2008, until the great financial crisis wherein the unemployment rate is increasing quadratically until the ended of the crisis ~2012. Afterwards, the trend decreases linearly, before an exponential spike during the COVID-19 pandemic. It seems to decrease at a linear rate since the end of the health crisis.

Secondly, we notice that there seems to be an obvious seasonal component to the time series, with a cyclical dip in unemployment during the summer.

From our initial observations, it does not seem immediately obvious that the volatility (variance) is non-constant at different levels; it seems to remain around the same level across different levels in the data.

To investigate all of these further and properly deal with this time series object, we will use additional tools to correctly identify the behavior and transform the series accordingly. Note that our path to a non-stationary object to a stationary one takes the following form:

3 Transformations

3.1 Variance

First, we will check for a non-constant variance. If detected, we need to treat it by changing the scale of our observations. To check we will use tools such as mean-variance plots and grouped histograms.

# mean-var
m <- apply(matrix(unempl[1:(21*12)],nr=12),2,mean)
v <- apply(matrix(unempl[1:(21*12)],nr=12),2,var)
plot(m,v,xlab="Yearly means",ylab="Yearly variances",main="UNEMPL")
abline(lm(v~m),col=2,lty=3,lwd=2)

seems to exist a positive relationship b/w the level and the variance; i.e., higher level corresponds to higher variance, on average.

To further investigate, we look at boxplots:

# grouped histogram
boxplot(unempl~floor(time(unempl)),
        col = "chocolate")

although not very obvious at first, since there seems to be higher variance at higher levels of our series, we decide to transform our data by scaling the ts points using a logarithmic function; we use log since (…).

# log trans
ln.unempl <- log(unempl)

par(mfrow=c(1,2))
boxplot(unempl~floor(time(unempl)), 
        xlab = "Year", 
        ylab = "UR (%)",
        main = "Unemployment in Spain, since 2000",
        col = "green4")
boxplot(ln.unempl~floor(time(ln.unempl)),
         xlab = "Year", 
        ylab = "log UR (%)",
        main = "Unemployment in Spain, since 2000",
        col = "maroon")
par(mfrow=c(1,1))
# mean-var
m <- apply(matrix(ln.unempl[1:(21*12)],nr=12),2,mean)
v <- apply(matrix(ln.unempl[1:(21*12)],nr=12),2,var)
plot(m,v,
     xlab="Yearly means",

     main="LOG UNEMPL",
     bg = "red4", col="black", pch = 21)
abline(lm(v~m),col=2,lty=3,lwd=2)

We see that indeed this transformation leads to a more constant variance

3.2 Seasonality

# check for seasonal cycles
par(mfrow=c(1,2))
monthplot(ln.unempl)
ts.plot(matrix(ln.unempl,nrow=12),col=1:8)
par(mfrow=c(1,1))

Seems to exist seasonality; we can further confirm by looking at a decomposition of our log transformed unemployed ts object:

# decomposition of our ts object
plot(decompose(ln.unempl))

We deal with the apparent seasonality in our ts by taking seasonal differences.

# seasonal diffs
d12ln.unempl=diff(ln.unempl,12)
plot(d12ln.unempl, main = "placeholder",
     ylab = "placeholder")
abline(h=0)
# sanity check
par(mfrow=c(1,2))
monthplot(d12ln.unempl)
ts.plot(matrix(d12ln.unempl,nrow=12),col=1:8)
par(mfrow=c(1,1))

We observe no signs of seasonal patterns after we take seasonal differences.

3.3 Mean

From our transformed ts object d12ln.unempl, we now need to finally check for a non-constant mean. Indeed, from our previous plot we notice that the trend seems to be non-constant; thus, we need to take first differences (\(\Delta (1-B)X_t\)) at every step iteratively, until our variance stops decreasing (to avoid ‘over-differencing’). Since at our first step our variance may not decrease, we will take first-differences three times, then compare their variances to see at which (if any) of these steps the variance increases.

par(mfrow=c(2,2))
# Regular Plot (accounting for seasonality and non-constant volatility)
plot(d12ln.unempl,
     main = "No Differences")
abline(h=0, col="blue")
abline(h=mean(d12ln.unempl), col = "red")

# Plot after taking first differences
d1d12ln.unempl <- diff(d12ln.unempl)

plot(d1d12ln.unempl,
     main = "First-Differences")
abline(h=0, col="blue")
abline(h=mean(d1d12ln.unempl), col = "red")

# Plot after taking second differences
d1d1d12ln.unempl <- diff(d1d12ln.unempl)

plot(d1d1d12ln.unempl,
     main = "Second-Differences")
abline(h=0, col="blue")
abline(h=mean(d1d1d12ln.unempl), col = "red")

# Plot after taking third differences
d1d1d1d12ln.unempl <- diff(d1d1d12ln.unempl)

plot(d1d1d1d12ln.unempl,
     main = "Third-Differences")
abline(h=0, col="blue")
abline(h=mean(d1d1d1d12ln.unempl), col = "red")
par(mfrow=c(1,1))
# Variance before first-diff
paste("Variance before 1st-diff: ", as.numeric(var(d12ln.unempl)))
[1] "Variance before 1st-diff:  0.0146746356418179"
# Variance at first-diff
paste("Variance after 1st-diff: ", as.numeric(var(d1d12ln.unempl)))
[1] "Variance after 1st-diff:  0.000349433996968396"
# Variance at second-diff
d1d1d12ln.unempl <- diff(d1d12ln.unempl)
paste("Variance after 2nd-diff: ", as.numeric(var(d1d1d12ln.unempl)))
[1] "Variance after 2nd-diff:  0.00032605296409322"
# Variance at third-diff
paste("Variance after 3rd-diff: ", as.numeric(var(d1d1d1d12ln.unempl)))
[1] "Variance after 3rd-diff:  0.000883633080628639"

Note that at the third step, our variance increases again. Thus, we take first-differences twice, then we stop after that. Thus, our final transformed ts object is now stationary, after applying a log transformation to deal with non-constant variance, a seasonal difference to deal with cyclical patterns, and two first-differences to deal with a non-constant mean.

4 ACF

4.1 Confirm Stationarity

To confirm the stationarity of our transformed ts object, we will look at the plots of the auto-correlation function pre- and post-transformation.

par(mfrow=c(1,2))
acf(unempl,
    main = "Pre-Transformation",
    col=c(2,rep(1,11))) # acf
acf(d1d1d12ln.unempl,
    main = "Post-Transformation",
    col=c(2,rep(1,11))) 
par(mfrow=c(1,1))

Note that for the post-transformation ACF, we notice that the ACF value immediately drops towards 0 as we go further back in time (higher lags). This evidence supports our transformation to a stationarity object; note that non-stationarity is evidenced by a gradual drop towards 0 as we increase t, which matches our pre-transformation ACF shape.

5 Model Proposition

To begin proposing candidate models for our data, we will look at the plots of both the auto-correlation and partial auto-correlation functions for our stationary object.

5.1 ACF & PACF

par(mfrow=c(1,2))

acf(d1d1d12ln.unempl,
    col=c(2,rep(1,11)),
    main = "d1d1d12ln.unempl") # acf
pacf(d1d1d12ln.unempl,
     col=c(rep(1,11),2),
     main = "d1d1d12ln.unempl") # pacf
par(mfrow=c(1,1))

To propose models, we look at the first non-zero values in the ACF & PACF. Note that when we select based on ACF, we are discussing MA(q) model values; for PACF, we are discussing AR(p) model values.

Looking at the ACF graph, I would propose MA(1) & MA(5) models since they contain all of our first non-zero values (i.e., outside or touching our 95% CI). This corresponds to an ARMA(1,0) and ARMA(5,0) model.

Looking at the PACF graph, I would propose AR(1) & AR(2) models.

Since we have both AR(1) and MA(1), and a high degree MA(5) model, I would suggest the following models:

  • ARMA(1,0)
  • ARMA(1,1)
  • ARMA(0,1)
  • ARMA(0,5)

Note that since we took seasonal differences during our transformation steps, the first seasonal non-zero component is at D=1. We want to only consider the first 5-6 lags for both the ACF/PACF graphs, since the closer we get to the seasonal components, the higher likelihood we encounter a “satellite” which would be heavily influenced by the seasonal component and perhaps show a false reading (a falsely identified non-zero lag).

We tend to favor more parsimonious models since they are more interpretable and typically decent approximations of reality, even if reality is highly non-linear (thanks to Taylor Series approximations). We like to consider a high order model in the ARMA(0,5) proposal, in case this fits very well; if this model overfits and has high variance (poor performance out of sample), we can always reduce the order and try simpler models.

6 Model Estimation

Note: During the estimation process, first specify the transformed stationary series (\(W_t\)) to obtain the mean estimation:

arima(w,order=c(p,0,q),seasonal=list(order=c(P,0,Q)))

If the mean is not significant, re-estimate the model for the original series (\(X_t\) or \(log(X_t)\)), specifying the differentiations in the estimation method:

arima(x,order=c(p,d,q),seasonal=list(order=c(P,D,Q)))

6.1 ARMA(1,0)

We start with our simplest AR(1) model specification. Note that since we took seasonal and first differences already, we are estimating a SARIMA model with the form:

\[ARIMA(1,0,0)(0,0,1)_{12}\]

           order=c(1,0,0),
           seasonal=list(order=c(0,0,1),

validation=function(model){
  s=frequency(get(model$series))
  resid=model$residuals
  par(mfrow=c(2,2),mar=c(3,3,3,3))
  #Residuals plot
  plot(resid,main="Residuals")
  abline(h=0)
  abline(h=c(-3*sd(resid),3*sd(resid)),lty=3,col=4)
  #Square Root of absolute values of residuals (Homocedasticity)
  scatter.smooth(sqrt(abs(resid)),main="Square Root of Absolute residuals",
                 lpars=list(col=2))
  
  #Normal plot of residuals
  qqnorm(resid)
  qqline(resid,col=2,lwd=2)
  
  ##Histogram of residuals with normal curve
  hist(resid,breaks=20,freq=FALSE)
  curve(dnorm(x,mean=mean(resid),sd=sd(resid)),col=2,add=T)
  
  
  #ACF & PACF of residuals
  par(mfrow=c(1,2))
  acf(resid,ylim=c(-1,1),lag.max=60,col=c(2,rep(1,s-1)),lwd=1)
  pacf(resid,ylim=c(-1,1),lag.max=60,col=c(rep(1,s-1),2),lwd=1)
  par(mfrow=c(1,1))
  
  #ACF & PACF of square residuals 
  par(mfrow=c(1,2))
  acf(resid^2,ylim=c(-1,1),lag.max=60,col=c(2,rep(1,s-1)),lwd=1)
  pacf(resid^2,ylim=c(-1,1),lag.max=60,col=c(rep(1,s-1),2),lwd=1)
  par(mfrow=c(1,1))
  
  #Ljung-Box p-values
  par(mar=c(2,2,1,1))
  tsdiag(model,gof.lag=7*s)
  cat("\n--------------------------------------------------------------------\n")
  print(model)
  
  #Stationary and Invertible
  cat("\nModul of AR Characteristic polynomial Roots: ", 
      Mod(polyroot(c(1,-model$model$phi))),"\n")
  cat("\nModul of MA Characteristic polynomial Roots: ",
      Mod(polyroot(c(1,model$model$theta))),"\n")
  
  #Model expressed as an MA infinity (psi-weights)
  psis=ARMAtoMA(ar=model$model$phi,ma=model$model$theta,lag.max=36)
  names(psis)=paste("psi",1:36)
  cat("\nPsi-weights (MA(inf))\n")
  cat("\n--------------------\n")
  print(psis[1:20])
  
  #Model expressed as an AR infinity (pi-weights)
  pis=-ARMAtoMA(ar=-model$model$theta,ma=-model$model$phi,lag.max=36)
  names(pis)=paste("pi",1:36)
  cat("\nPi-weights (AR(inf))\n")
  cat("\n--------------------\n")
  print(pis[1:20])
 
  cat("\nNormality Tests\n")
  cat("\n--------------------\n")
 
  ##Shapiro-Wilks Normality test
  print(shapiro.test(resid(model)))

  suppressMessages(require(nortest,quietly=TRUE,warn.conflicts=FALSE))
  ##Anderson-Darling test
  print(ad.test(resid(model)))
  
  suppressMessages(require(tseries,quietly=TRUE,warn.conflicts=FALSE))
  ##Jarque-Bera test
  print(jarque.bera.test(resid(model)))
  
  cat("\nHomoscedasticity Test\n")
  cat("\n--------------------\n")
  suppressMessages(require(lmtest,quietly=TRUE,warn.conflicts=FALSE))
  ##Breusch-Pagan test
  obs=get(model$series)
  print(bptest(resid(model)~I(obs-resid(model))))
  
  cat("\nIndependence Tests\n")
  cat("\n--------------------\n")
  
  ##Ljung-Box test
  cat("\nLjung-Box test\n")
  print(t(apply(matrix(c(1:4,(1:4)*s)),1,function(el) {
    te=Box.test(resid(model),type="Ljung-Box",lag=el)
    c(lag=(te$parameter),statistic=te$statistic[[1]],p.value=te$p.value)})))

}
