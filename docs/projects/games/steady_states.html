A general equilibrium model of two-agent, two-good trade
Author

1. Introduction

Imagine two local vendors go to a farmer’s market to sell their goods. Zack, a local apple farmer, had an exceptional yield this year at his family-owned orchard. Yuri, on the other hand, is an experienced fishmonger who specializes in selling seasonal varieties of seafood. Each of the two vendors start out with a their own baskets of goods and value their goods according to their own preferences. Zack, as much as he loves apples, prefers to hold a more balanced basket of both apples and fish so he has more possibilities come dinnertime; Yuri feels the same way. Both therefore would benefit from trading their goods with one another; however, how do we know which trades are worth making? When are there no further gains possible from trading with one another? Can a market can find that outcome on its own, just by agents acting in their own self-interests?

These are major questions behind general equilibrium theory in microeconomics. This project uses MATLAB to investigate these questions in a simple exchange economy with two agents, two goods, and fixed endowments. The goal is to better understand many of the abstract concepts that microeconomic theory proposes under specific conditions.

The project is organized around five questions:

  1. Which allocations are efficient, given the agents’ resources and preferences?
  2. Which of those allocations would both agents voluntarily accept?
  3. Can a competitive market find an equilibrium allocation?
  4. Does the price-adjustment process converge when it starts from a poor initial guess?
  5. Can we visualize the process and see how the economy moves toward equilibrium?

Each question introduces a different part of the model. Together, they show how a set of theoretical results can be translated into numerical methods, tested across different preference structures, and brought to life through visualization.

2. The Economic Setup

2.1 Agents, Goods, and Preferences

The starting point is the agents’ preferences. An agent does not need to assign an objective value to every basket of goods; they only need to be able to rank the alternatives. A utility function \(u_i(x_1,x_2)\) provides a convenient numerical representation of that ranking. If an agent prefers basket \((A)\) to basket \((B)\), then \(u_i(A)>u_i(B)\). The numerical values themselves are not meaningful in isolation: utility is ordinal, so any strictly increasing transformation represents the same preferences.

The standard model assumes that preferences are complete, transitive, and continuous. Completeness means that the agent can compare any two baskets, while transitivity rules out contradictory rankings. Continuity ensures that small changes in a basket do not produce abrupt changes in the preference ordering. Local non-satiation adds another important condition: however good a basket may be, there is always a nearby basket the agent strictly prefers. This assumption will matter later when we consider whether agents spend their entire budget.

Convexity is often added as a further assumption. It captures a preference for balanced combinations over extreme ones and helps ensure that the usual first-order conditions identify utility-maximizing allocations. But not every preference structure fits this textbook case. To see where the standard methods work and where they fail, the solver handles four broad types of preferences: smooth quasi-concave utilities, Leontief preferences with kinks, convex preferences represented by a max function, and non-quasi-concave utilities for which an interior tangency can be a minimum rather than a maximum.

The running example uses two agents, Yuri and Zack, who value apples and fish according to

\[ u_{\text{Yuri}}(x_1,x_2)=x_1(x_2+3), \qquad u_{\text{Zack}}(x_1,x_2)=x_1(x_2+2). \]

Here, \((x_1)\) denotes apples and \((x_2)\) denotes fish. Each utility function describes one agent’s preferences over their own basket. The functions are similar, but not identical: the difference in their constants creates different marginal rates of substitution and therefore gives the agents a reason to trade.

2.2 Endowments and the Edgeworth Box

An endowment is the basket an agent owns before trading begins. It determines the agent’s starting point and, as we will see in Section 4, the outside option against which any proposed trade must be evaluated.

Let Yuri’s endowment be \((\omega_{\text{Yuri}})\) and Zack’s be \((\omega_{\text{Zack}})\). The total resources available to the economy are

\[ \omega=\omega_{\text{Yuri}}+\omega_{\text{Zack}}. \]

These resources remain fixed throughout the model. Every apple or fish Yuri gives up must end up with Zack, and vice versa. Trade changes who owns the goods, not how many goods exist.

The Edgeworth box is a convenient way to represent this constraint. Yuri’s allocation is measured from the bottom-left corner, while Zack’s is measured from the top-right corner, with his axes reversed. The box’s width represents the total supply of apples and its height represents the total supply of fish. Any point inside the box therefore identifies both agents’ holdings at once, with no goods created or lost.

For the running example, both agents begin with equal endowments:

\[ \omega_{\text{Yuri}}=\omega_{\text{Zack}}=(5,5), \qquad \omega=(10,10). \]

Note

Every point in the Edgeworth box represents a feasible allocation of the economy’s resources. The total supply of each good is fixed; only its distribution between the two agents changes.

2.3 The Marginal Rate of Substitution

The marginal rate of substitution (MRS) describes how much of one good an agent is willing to give up for a small increase in another while remaining on the same indifference curve. It is the local slope of that curve and, in economic terms, the agent’s willingness to trade one good for the other.

For differentiable utility functions,

\[ MRS=\frac{MU(x_1)}{MU(x_2)} =\frac{\partial u/\partial x_1}{\partial u/\partial x_2}. \]

In the running example, the marginal rates of substitution are

\[ MRS_{\text{Yuri}}=\frac{x_2+3}{x_1}, \qquad MRS_{\text{Zack}}=\frac{x_2+2}{x_1}. \]

The important point is not the formula itself, but what happens when the two rates differ. If Yuri and Zack value an additional apple differently relative to an additional fish, there may be an exchange that benefits both. The agent who values apples more at the margin is willing to give up more fish to obtain them, while the other agent is willing to make the opposite trade.

As the allocation changes, both agents’ marginal rates change. Under the standard differentiability and interior-solution conditions, mutually beneficial trade ends when their MRS values coincide. This gives us the central condition used to derive the Pareto set in the next section.

3. Pareto Optimality

3.1 The Concept

An allocation is Pareto optimal if there is no feasible alternative that makes at least one agent strictly better off without making the other worse off. The definition is deliberately limited: it rules out allocations where mutually beneficial improvements remain possible, but it does not tell us whether the outcome is fair or whether either agent would voluntarily accept it.

This distinction matters. An allocation where one agent owns everything and the other owns nothing can still be Pareto optimal. Taking a good away from the first agent to give it to the second would make the first worse off, so no Pareto improvement is possible. Efficiency alone therefore does not tell us which allocations are reasonable outcomes of voluntary trade.

For smooth preferences and an interior allocation, Pareto optimality can often be identified geometrically. The agents’ indifference curves must be tangent. If the curves cross, there is typically a lens-shaped region in which both agents can obtain higher utility. A move into that region is a Pareto improvement. At a tangency, the curves have the same local slope, so this particular opportunity for mutual improvement disappears.

3.2 The MRS Condition

For an interior allocation, the standard tangency condition is

\[ MRS_{\text{Yuri}}(x_1,x_2) = MRS_{\text{Zack}}(\omega_1-x_1,\omega_2-x_2). \]

Writing Zack’s utility in terms of Yuri’s holdings is a useful simplification. Once Yuri’s allocation \((x_1,x_2)\)is known, Zack’s follows from the resource constraint:

\[ (x_{1,\text{Zack}},x_{2,\text{Zack}}) =(\omega_1-x_1,\omega_2-x_2). \]

The problem can therefore be represented in a single coordinate system rather than solving for four quantities independently.

When the MRS values differ, there is scope for a mutually beneficial marginal trade. When they are equal, the agents agree on the local exchange rate. However, the tangency condition is not a complete solution in every case. It relies on differentiability and suitable curvature properties, and it can miss corner solutions, kinks, or interior points that are minima rather than maxima. The solver needs to account for these possibilities explicitly.

3.3 Special Preference Cases

The same first-order condition cannot be applied mechanically to every utility function. The shape of the preferences changes the geometry of the solution and, in some cases, the appropriate numerical method.

These cases illustrate why the solver is built around a decision process rather than one universal formula. Smooth, well-behaved utilities can often be handled with derivatives. Kinked preferences need a different approach, since the usual first-order conditions do not apply at the kink. Non-quasi-concave preferences create another problem: an interior tangency can satisfy the first-order condition without being a utility maximum. The solver needs to account for all three cases, rather than treating every preference structure the same way.

3.4 The Solver Decision Tree

The dispatcher in solveParetoSet.m chooses a method based on the properties of the utility functions. It first checks whether both functions are differentiable. If either contains a min or max, the problem goes to paretoNonDiff, which handles the non-differentiable case separately.

For differentiable utilities, the solver checks for neutral-good cases, where marginal utility is zero or becomes infinite at the relevant point. Depending on the combination, the Pareto set may collapse to an edge or a single point. It then checks for perfect substitutes, where the marginal rate of substitution is constant. If both agents have constant MRS, the relationship between those rates determines whether the efficient set covers a larger part of the box or is restricted to its boundary.

The next check concerns curvature. In this implementation, the bordered-Hessian test is used to flag cases that may be non-quasi-concave. Rather than relying on an interior tangency in those cases, the solver calls paretoBoundary. If none of the special cases applies, it uses the standard approach: find interior tangencies, check the corners, and filter the candidate allocations.

% solveParetoSet.m : top-level dispatch
if mrs.diff1 && mrs.diff2
    % Neutral-good edge cases
    if     mrs.MRS1_zero && mrs.MRS2_inf,  px1 = 0;        px2 = omega(2); return
    elseif mrs.MRS1_inf  && mrs.MRS2_zero, px1 = omega(1); px2 = 0;        return
    elseif mrs.MRS1_zero || mrs.MRS1_inf || mrs.MRS2_zero || mrs.MRS2_inf
        % Single-agent neutrality: route to the appropriate edge
    end

    % Perfect substitutes: constant MRS on both sides
    if mrs.constant_MRS1 && mrs.constant_MRS2
        % Handle the constant-MRS case
        return
    end

    % Bordered-Hessian test: flag non-quasi-concave preferences
    if flags.convex_pref
        [px1, px2] = paretoBoundary(u1, u2_sub, omega, x1_range, x2_range);
        return
    end

    % Standard case: interior tangency + corner solutions
    [int_x1, int_x2] = paretoInterior(mrs.MRS1, mrs.MRS2_linked, omega, ...);
    [cor_x1, cor_x2] = paretoCorners(mrs.MRS1, mrs.MRS2_linked, omega, ...);
    [px1, px2] = paretoFilter([int_x1, cor_x1], [int_x2, cor_x2], ...);

else
    % At least one utility is non-differentiable (min or max)
    [px1, px2, flags] = paretoNonDiff(u1, u2_sub, omega, x1_range);
end

The paretoBoundary method handles cases where a closed-form tangency solution may not be reliable. It checks candidate allocations along the edges of the Edgeworth box against a \(100\times100\) grid of feasible interior allocations. A candidate is kept only if no other point makes both agents at least as well off and one of them strictly better off.

The numerical check follows the definition of Pareto dominance:

% paretoBoundary.m : inner dominance check
% u1g, u2g are flattened utility values across the grid.
% u1c, u2c are the utilities at a candidate allocation.

tol = 1e-8;

dominates = (u1g >= u1c - tol) & ...
            (u2g >= u2c - tol) & ...
            ((u1g > u1c + tol) | (u2g > u2c + tol));

is_efficient = ~any(dominates);

This is a numerical approximation rather than a proof that every efficient allocation has been found. Its accuracy depends on the grid resolution, tolerance, and treatment of utility values near domain boundaries. It is useful for checking difficult cases, but results should be interpreted with those limitations in mind.

4. The Core

4.1 From Efficiency to Individual Rationality

The Pareto set includes every allocation where one agent cannot be made better off without making the other worse off. It does not, by itself, say whether either agent would prefer such an allocation to their initial endowment.

The core adds that requirement. Given endowments \(w_1\) and \(w_2\), an allocation must be Pareto efficient and give both agents at least as much utility as they receive at their endowments. The individual-rationality conditions are

\[ u_1(x_1,x_2) \geq u_1(w_1), \qquad u_2(\omega_1-x_1,\omega_2-x_2) \geq u_2(w_2), \]

where \(\omega=w_1+w_2\)is the economy’s total endowment.

The implementation evaluates these conditions along the Pareto set. Allocations that fail either condition are removed, leaving the part of the efficient set that both agents would accept. The resulting set is the core for this two-agent exchange economy.

4.2 Implementation

The core function reuses the Pareto set if it is already available in the incoming state structure. It calculates each agent’s utility at their endowment, evaluates utility along the efficient allocations, and applies the individual-rationality constraints.

% core.m -- simplified individual-rationality filter

u1_outside = u1(w1(1), w1(2));
u2_outside = u2(w2(1), w2(2));

% Utilities at candidate allocations
u1_candidates = u1(px1, px2);
u2_candidates = u2(omega(1) - px1, omega(2) - px2);

tol = 1e-8;

ir1 = u1_candidates >= u1_outside - tol;
ir2 = u2_candidates >= u2_outside - tol;

ir = ir1 & ir2;

cx1 = px1(ir);
cx2 = px2(ir);

The result is a useful distinction between efficiency and voluntary participation. An allocation can lie on the Pareto set but outside the core because one agent would rather keep their original endowment. The endowment therefore determines which part of the efficient set is individually rational.

5. Competitive Equilibrium

5.1 Prices and Budget Constraints

Pareto efficiency and the core describe allocations without specifying a market mechanism. Meanwhile, competitive equilibrium introduces prices. Each agent takes prices as given and chooses the most preferred affordable basket, subject to their budget constraint.

Let \(p=(p_1,p_2)\)be the vector of prices. Agent $i)’s wealth comes from the market value of their endowment:

\[ m_i=p_1w_{i1}+p_2w_{i2}. \]

The agent’s optimization problem is

\[ \max_{x_{i1},x_{i2}} u_i(x_{i1},x_{i2}) \]

subject to

\[ p_1x_{i1}+p_2x_{i2}\leq m_i, \qquad x_{i1},x_{i2}\geq 0. \]

A competitive equilibrium requires both agents to optimize at the same prices and the resulting allocation to clear the markets. For each good, aggregate demand must equal aggregate supply.

\[ x_{11}+x_{21}=\omega_1, \qquad x_{12}+x_{22}=\omega_2. \]

The equilibrium price ratio coordinates the agents’ choices. At an interior optimum with differentiable preferences, the MRS equals the relative price:

\[ MRS_i=\frac{p_1}{p_2}. \]

Since both agents face the same prices, their MRS values coincide at an interior competitive equilibrium. This connects the agents’ individual optimization problems to the market-clearing conditions.

5.2 Solving for Equilibrium

The equilibrium solver searches for prices and allocations that satisfy the agents’ demand conditions and clear both markets. Since only relative prices matter in a standard exchange economy, one price can be normalized. The implementation uses a relative price parameter to reduce the problem to a smaller numerical search. The solver must also account for corner solutions. If an agent’s optimal demand lies on the boundary of their budget set, the interior first-order conditions need not hold with equality. The implementation therefore compares candidate solutions and checks their feasibility, budget constraints, and market-clearing errors.

The demand functions are derived from the utility functions and the agents’ budget constraints. The resulting demands are then evaluated at candidate prices. A candidate equilibrium is retained only when the allocations are feasible and the aggregate excess demand is sufficiently close to zero.

% equilibrium residuals
excess1 = x_A(1) + x_B(1) - omega(1);
excess2 = x_A(2) + x_B(2) - omega(2);

market_clearing_error = max(abs([excess1, excess2]));

The residuals provide a direct check on whether the computed allocation clears the markets or not.

5.3 Welfare Checks

The first welfare theorem states, under its standard assumptions, that a competitive equilibrium allocation is Pareto efficient. The second welfare theorem gives a converse under additional conditions: an efficient allocation can be decentralized as a competitive equilibrium after a redistribution of initial endowments.

The model uses welfare checks to compare the computed equilibrium with the Pareto set and the core. These comparisons are useful for identifying inconsistencies between the numerical routines. They should not be, however, treated as a general proof of either welfare theorem as we also consider preferences that violate some of the standard assumptions.

The implementation records whether the equilibrium lies on the computed Pareto set and whether each agent receives at least their endowment utility.

6. Tâtonnement and Price Adjustment

6.1 The Idea

Finding an equilibrium numerically tells us which prices clear the markets, but it does not explain how a market might approach those prices from a different starting point. Tâtonnement provides a simple way to investigate that question.

In the model, an auctioneer announces a relative price. Each agent responds with their demand at that price, and the economy’s excess demand is calculated. The price is then adjusted according to the imbalance between demand and supply. If demand for a good exceeds its available supply, its relative price rises; if supply exceeds demand, the price moves in the opposite direction.

For a two-good exchange economy, the process can be represented using the excess demand for good 1. With good 2 as the numeraire, the update rule takes the form:

\[ p_{t+1}=\max\left(\varepsilon,\, p_t+\alpha z_1(p_t)\right), \]

where \(p_t\) is the relative price at iteration \(t\), \(z_1(p_t)\) is excess demand for good 1, \(\alpha\) is the adjustment step size, and \(\varepsilon>0\) prevents the price from becoming non-positive.

The exact behavior depends on the demand functions, the step size, and the initial price. A large step can cause oscillation or divergence, whereas a small step may produce slower adjustment. Convergence is therefore not guaranteed and should be checked (rather than assumed).

6.2 The Simulation Loop

The simulation repeatedly evaluates the agents’ demands and updates the price. The implementation stores the price path and associated quantities so that the process can be inspected after the run.

% tâtonnement loop
p = p0;
p_path = nan(max_iter, 1);
z_path = nan(max_iter, 1);

for t = 1:max_iter
    xA = dFn_A(p);
    xB = dFn_B(p);

    z1 = xA(1) + xB(1) - omega(1);

    p_path(t) = p;
    z_path(t) = z1;

    if abs(z1) < tol
        p_path = p_path(1:t);
        z_path = z_path(1:t);
        break
    end

    p = max(eps_price, p + alpha * z1);
end

6.3 Monte Carlo Experiments

A single simulation only shows what happens from one initial price. However, to assess sensitivity to the starting point, the project runs the adjustment process repeatedly from a range of initial prices.

The Monte Carlo routine records the final price and whether the run converged. The convergence rate is the proportion of tested starting prices that satisfy the stopping criterion within the iteration limit.

\[ \text{Convergence rate} = \frac{\text{Number of converged runs}} {\text{Total number of runs}}. \]

The resulting distribution shows whether convergence is robust to the initial price or depends strongly on where the process begins. It can also reveal cases in which the update rule oscillates, approaches a boundary, or fails to converge within the available iterations.

7. Visualizing the Economy

7.1 Bringing the Model Together

The visualization brings the theoretical objects and numerical results into one place. The Edgeworth box shows the feasible allocations, the Pareto set identifies the efficient ones, the core highlights the individually rational region, and the competitive equilibrium marks the allocation supported by prices.

The animated simulation adds a time dimension: as the relative price changes, the agents’ demands and resulting allocations change alongside it. The visualization makes it easier to see whether the simulated economy moves toward the equilibrium allocation or follows a less stable path.

8. Technical Architecture

8.1 The State Chain

The project is divided into functions that each handle one part of the economic model. The Pareto-set routine runs first, followed by the core, competitive equilibrium, simulations, and visualization. To avoid repeating work, each function can receive a state structure containing results computed by earlier stages.

The workflow looks like this:

s = paretoSet(u1, u2, omega);
s = core(u1, u2, w1, w2, 'from', s);
s = walrasian(u1, u2, w1, w2, 'from', s);
s = sims(u1, u2, w1, w2, 'from', s);
s = viz(u1, u2, w1, w2, 'from', s, 'style', 'stacked');

Each call extends the structure s with the outputs needed by later stages.

Each function uses an arguments block to define its inputs and defaults. The optional from argument carries the state produced upstream. If a required field already exists, the function reuses it; otherwise, it computes that result itself.

function s = walrasian(u1, u2, w1, w2, options)
arguments
    u1
    u2
    w1 (1,2) {mustBeNumeric}
    w2 (1,2) {mustBeNumeric}
    options.p0     (1,1) double = 1.0
    options.target (1,2) double = [NaN NaN]
    options.from   struct       = struct()
end

% Reuse the Pareto set if it was computed upstream
if isfield(options.from, 'px1')
    px1   = options.from.px1;
    px2   = options.from.px2;
    flags = options.from.flags;
    mrs   = options.from.mrs;
else
    % Compute the required Pareto-set results here
end

% ... walrasian-specific work ...

s      = options.from;
s.p_eq = p_eq;
s.x_A  = x_A;
s.x_B  = x_B;
end

This design makes functions usable individually or as a pipeline. Running walrasian without upstream state should produce the same economic result as running it after paretoSet and core, although it may repeat calculations. The state chain separates the logic and avoids unnecessary duplicate work.

9. Limitations and Extensions

9.1 Current Limitations

The project uses a small, closed economy in order to see the relationship between preferences, feasible allocations, prices, and welfare in a clear way; however, it limits what the model can say about real markets.

  • Fixed resources: The economy is an exchange economy. There is no production, investment, or capital accumulation.
  • Two agents and two goods: The Edgeworth box is especially useful for this setting, but its geometry does not scale directly to larger economies.
  • Price-taking behaviour: Agents treat prices as given and have no market power.
  • Numerical approximations: Some preference cases rely on grids, tolerances, and numerical optimization. Their results depend on the resolution and robustness of those methods.
  • Stylized price adjustment: Tâtonnement is an analytical mechanism for studying price convergence, not a complete model of how actual markets operate.

9.2 Natural Extensions

Several extensions follow naturally from the existing architecture.

  • Many-agent tâtonnement: Aggregate excess demand across more agents and examine whether the same price-adjustment rule converges.
  • Overlapping generations: Allow agents to trade across time periods, introducing intertemporal budgets and the role of saving.
  • Network trade: Replace the centralized auctioneer with bilateral trades along a network, making it possible to study how the pattern of connections affects the path toward an efficient allocation.

The network extension is particularly interesting because it changes the process of trade without necessarily changing the underlying preferences. Instead of assuming that all agents can respond to one common price, the model could examine how local interactions and limited access to trading partners affect convergence and the allocations that are ultimately reached.

10. Conclusion

The project began with five questions about a simple exchange economy. The Pareto set identifies allocations where no further Pareto improvement is possible. The core adds individual rationality, narrowing the efficient set to allocations that both agents would accept. Competitive equilibrium introduces prices as a way to coordinate individual choices, while tâtonnement provides a mechanism for testing whether those prices can be reached from different starting points. Finally, the visualization links the theoretical objects to the path followed by the simulated economy.

Implementing the model also makes clear that these concepts are related but not interchangeable. An efficient allocation need not be fair, and Pareto efficiency alone does not establish that every agent prefers the outcome to their endowment. Likewise, a numerical routine that appears to converge is not necessarily solving the intended economic problem. The agents’ demands, budget constraints, market-clearing conditions, and welfare comparisons all need to align under specific conditions for results to hold.
